Methodology

How AgroTwin decides what to suggest, and how we know it is honest

A farmer choosing next season's crops is betting a year of water, soil and income. AgroTwin doesn't guess the future. It replays the past thirty-odd years of real NASA weather over your field, for your current rotation and for the alternatives, and shows how each would have done, good years and bad.

01What goes in

NASA POWER gives daily temperature, rain, humidity, wind and sunshine for any point on Earth since 1981. From those we compute how much water a well-watered crop would use each day (the FAO-56 reference evapotranspiration, the method irrigation engineers use worldwide). MODIS satellites add the field's greenness every 16 days since 2000, and its actual water use. SoilGrids gives the soil texture. On the map you can also see SMAP soil moisture and GPM IMERG rainfall straight from NASA.

02The field as a water tank

Every day: the tank gains rain (minus what runs off) and irrigation, and loses what the crop uses and what drains away. When it falls below the stress line, the crop uses less water than it wants, and its yield drops.

We run that bookkeeping day by day for the crop's actual growth stages. The crop drinks little after sowing, most at flowering, less near harvest (FAO-56 crop coefficients). An irrigated field is refilled whenever it drops to the stress line; a rain-fed field never is. The model keeps exact books: water in minus water out equals the change in the tank, to within a millionth of a millimetre, and a test checks it.

Yield follows the FAO-33 rule: the share of yield lost is proportional to the share of water the crop was short of, plus a penalty for very hot days at flowering. Profit is yield × price − costs, using 19 published Egyptian prices and costs and 21 clearly marked estimates you can edit.

03Replaying every year since 1991

Instead of one "average year", we run each rotation through every three-year stretch of real weather since 1991: 32 stretches. Each one becomes one stripe in the Harvest Barcode: green when it paid, amber when the margin was thin, red when it lost money. You see at a glance how often a rotation would have worked, and when it would have failed.

Before replaying, each past year is warmed to today's climate by the warming trend measured at that very field (about +0.3 °C per decade in the Delta). Old years then behave like this year would, not like 1991 did.

About forty candidate rotations are searched; your current one and the two best different ones are shown, ranked by the priorities you choose: water, soil, profit, or avoiding bad years.

04The robot narrows the range

A satellite pixel is kilometres wide; the Verdex robot stands in your field. As it measures soil moisture through the season, AgroTwin asks which past years left the soil looking like this, and trusts those more. The range of possible outcomes for this season gets narrower.

How do we know the narrower range is honest? The twin experiment: we hide one past year, pretend the robot measured it, and check whether the narrowed range still contains what really happened. Repeated for every year. Without a correction it didn't hold up (soil moisture is slow to change, so daily readings are not independent), so we measure the soil's "memory" from the data and calibrate for it. Readings never change the forecast for future seasons; we tested that too, and it made things worse.

"Better" compares the forecast's error (CRPS, the standard score for range forecasts) with the error of simply repeating last season. The comparison is against modelled outcomes on real weather, not measured harvests, which don't exist for these fields.

06Our own model: Field Pulse

Connecting to NASA's data is the easy part. Field Pulse is a model we trained ourselves, and it answers a question no single dataset can: is this field as green as the weather says it should be?

It is trained on about 1,200 real Nile Delta cropland pixels: every 16-day MODIS image since 2018, read from NASA's GIBS service by decoding NASA's official colour map back into NDVI (checked against the original MODIS values: error 0.002), plus 12 fields from NASA's ORNL archive back to 2015. For each image we gave the model three kinds of input: the field's own recent greenness and its usual season; the last two months of NASA POWER weather; and a physics estimate, where the same water-tank bookkeeping as section 02 is run with a crop size read from the satellite, giving the crop's water demand and how stressed it would be on rain alone. A gradient-boosted tree model then learns how greenness changes 16 and 32 days ahead, and gives a range rather than a single number.

Why a farmer cares. When a new image comes in below the model's range, the weather and the season don't explain the drop, so something on the field might: pests, salinity, a missed irrigation. That is where the Verdex robot should go first. Satellites cover every field cheaply; the robot only visits the ones Field Pulse flags.

07Ask it out loud

A farmer can tap the microphone and ask in Egyptian Arabic: «أزرع إيه السنة الجاية؟», «أروي امتى؟», «أرضي عاملة إيه؟». The speech is turned into text on the laptop itself (Whisper, open source; no audio is sent anywhere), or by the browser. The assistant then calls the same engines as the page (the replay, the 16-day water planner, Field Pulse, the time machine, the quoted prices) and answers out loud with an Egyptian neural voice.

Without an internet connection or an AI key, a rule-based router understands the common questions. With a Claude key, Claude chooses which engines to call, and a checker refuses any number in its answer that no engine produced; if it finds one, the rule-based answer is used instead. Under each answer, the app lists the engines it used.

08Plan Mode: plan by talking

Press Plan with AI and talk. The assistant asks a few questions (crops, area, salinity, how often canal water runs short, what matters most), then searches every candidate rotation × summer sowing date (−14, 0, +14 days) × water strategy (full or 85% summer irrigation) through the same physics, with price swings, canal shortfalls and FAO salt tolerance included, and draws the plan: crops, sowing and harvest dates, water, yields, profit ranges and every option it tried on a water-versus-profit chart. Say “keep rice”, “less water” or “I have 3 feddans” and it re-plans in about two seconds. It remembers the farm. With a Claude key, Claude runs the conversation and calls the plan engine; a checker rejects any number the engine did not produce. Without one, a fast rule engine for Egyptian Arabic and English runs offline.

We also tested whether last summer's NASA POWER heat and soil wetness predict the coming season well enough to favour some past years over others. They did not (−0.5% skill), so all past years keep equal weight. Our water engine was checked against NASA's independent MODIS ET product: it follows the seasonal timing (monthly r = 0.59 irrigated, 0.88 rain-fed) but runs higher than MODIS over irrigated fields, a known limitation of that product in deserts; both are reported.

09Things a farm twin usually can't do

Your field's past, read from orbit. MODIS shows which seasons your field was green. AgroTwin replays each of them on that year's weather and tells you what the suggested rotation would have saved and earned since 2015, without asking you a single question about your history.

Canal mode. Delta farms share canal water, and the hardest week is mid-summer. AgroTwin plans the farms on one canal together: each stays within ten points of its own best plan, and the summer sowing dates are spread over a month so the canal's worst week is much lighter. In the demo canal the peak week drops by more than half.

Next 16 days. The same water bookkeeping continues on the weather forecast, so the advice ends with a date: "irrigate around Sunday, about 220 m³ per feddan".

10Honesty rules

  • Every number on screen can be tapped to see where it came from: NASA, computed, the robot, a quoted source, or a named assumption.
  • A badge always says whether robot data is live, a replay, or simulated.
  • The AI advisers may only use numbers the engine computed; a checker blocks anything else.
  • A list of what satellites cannot see (salinity, nematodes, water rights) comes with every plan, for a local agronomist to check.
  • History is not a forecast. AgroTwin shows ranges, not promises, and the decision stays with the farmer.
